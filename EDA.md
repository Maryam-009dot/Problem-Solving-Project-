Chart 1: Pakistan's child mortality fell by about 45% since 2000, but it now sits near the top of its peer group 
Pakistan's under-5 mortality fell steadily from 107.9 in 2000 to 59.7 in 2022. Almost every peer also fell over these years, so Pakistan's improvement is part of a general trend. In 2000 six peers were above Pakistan, but by 2022 only two lines remain above it, which means Pakistan lost ground relative to its peers. The highest line (Nigeria) is almost flat after 2010 at about 117. A few peers show short spikes, so the series are mostly smooth but not perfectly so.

Chart 2: Taking the log turns the right-skewed GDP distribution into a roughly symmetric one
Before the log, GDP per capita is right-skewed: most country-years lie between about 1,500 and 7,000 dollars, with a long tail up to about 16,000. After the log, the histogram is much more symmetric. The skewness fell from about 1.88 to about -0.13

hart 3: Richer country-years have lower mortality, but countries at the same income differ a lot
Mortality falls as income rises, quickly at low income and more slowly at high income, so the relationship is curved, not a straight line. The dots form separate tracks, one per country, which shows that each country moves down the curve over time and that countries sit on different curves. At the same income level (for example log GDP around 8.5) mortality ranges from about 25 to more than 150, so income alone does not explain mortality. Pakistan's red dots sit in the upper part of the cloud: at its income level, many peers have lower mortality. This is the gap my project studies 

Chart 5: Health spending shows no clear link to mortality, and splitting by income does not change that
asked whether the relationship between health spending and mortality changes when countries are split by income. In both halves the points form a wide cloud with no clear downward line. Countries spending only 3 to 5% of GDP have mortality from under 10 to more than 150.
